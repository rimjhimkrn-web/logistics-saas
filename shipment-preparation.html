Prepare with care · Move with confidence

Every shipment starts before pickup.

Practical guidance to prepare, pack, protect, label and document cargo before collection—so the shipment is safer to handle and easier to identify throughout its journey.

These guidelines are a practical baseline for ordinary cargo. Packaging requirements depend on the goods, fragility, weight, shape, stacking, handling equipment, weather exposure, route, mode and carrier. A shipment may need engineered packaging, specialist handling or a provider inspection. Where instructions differ, follow the stricter lawful requirement and obtain written clarification before tendering the cargo.

1. Scope and responsibility

The shipper is generally best placed to understand the goods and arrange suitable preparation before collection. The exact contractual allocation of packing, loading, securing and documentation duties must be checked in the accepted quote, booking and transport documents.

Do not assume that a driver, carrier, warehouse or KRIM will repack, inspect, count, weigh or certify the contents unless that service is expressly included in writing. This guide is not a packaging engineering specification and cannot replace specialist advice for high-risk cargo.

2. Before you start packing

  1. Confirm the exact pickup and delivery addresses, access restrictions, operating hours and contact persons.
  2. Confirm the accepted transport mode, vehicle/equipment type, handling method and any provider-specific preparation instructions.
  3. Identify the goods accurately, including material, quantity, fragility, liquid content, batteries, hazardous components and any special handling needs.
  4. Check whether the goods are prohibited, restricted, dangerous, temperature-sensitive, high-value, oversized or otherwise conditional under the Prohibited & Restricted Cargo Policy.
  5. Check packaging strength, dimensions, weight, stackability, lifting points and weather protection against the actual journey.
  6. Obtain any required written approval before packing or dispatch where the goods need special acceptance.

3. Choose packaging suited to the goods and journey

Use new or serviceable packaging that is structurally sound, clean, dry and appropriate for the cargo's weight and vulnerability. The outer container should resist expected compression, vibration, handling and environmental exposure without collapsing or opening.

Cartons & boxes

Use a box with adequate strength, intact seams and enough internal space for cushioning without allowing movement.

Crates & cases

Use robust construction for heavy, fragile or high-value items; provide stable bases and suitable internal restraints.

Bags & sacks

Use material and closures suited to the product; protect against tearing, leakage, moisture and dust release.

Drums & containers

Use compatible, correctly rated containers and closures; hazardous contents require applicable certified packaging and rules.

Do not exceed the packaging manufacturer's load rating. Avoid damaged, wet, weakened or previously contaminated packaging. Reuse only when it remains fit for purpose and all old labels or hazard markings are correctly removed or replaced.

4. Protect and immobilise the contents

  • Wrap each fragile item separately and use cushioning to absorb shock on all sides.
  • Fill voids so items cannot shift, collide or puncture the outer packaging during normal handling.
  • Separate heavy items from delicate items; distribute weight evenly and keep the centre of gravity stable.
  • Protect sharp edges, protrusions and corners so they cannot injure handlers or damage adjacent freight.
  • Use moisture barriers or desiccants only where suitable for the goods and journey; do not trap moisture against products that may be damaged by condensation.
  • Bag liquids with appropriate leak containment and upright protection where lawful and accepted; never use ordinary packing as a substitute for regulated dangerous-goods packaging.
  • Protect electronics against impact, static discharge and moisture using suitable materials.

For machinery or equipment, secure moving parts, drain or isolate fluids only as instructed by the manufacturer and applicable rules, and protect exposed controls, connectors and precision surfaces.

5. Close, seal and secure each package

Use a closure method and tape/strapping appropriate to the package weight and construction. Seal all openings and reinforce seams and corners where required. Strapping must be tensioned safely and must not crush the goods or create sharp exposed ends.

Use tamper-evident seals where the contract, cargo risk or provider requires them. Record seal numbers on the relevant documents and ensure they match at handover. A seal is a security aid, not proof by itself that the contents are correct or undamaged.

6. Labels, addresses and handling marks

  • Show the correct recipient name, complete delivery address, postal code, reachable phone number and shipment reference on a durable label.
  • Place labels on a flat, visible surface; avoid seams, openings, straps and corners.
  • Remove or fully cover obsolete labels, barcodes and routing instructions.
  • Use orientation, fragile, keep-dry, temperature or lifting marks only when accurate and relevant; do not rely on symbols instead of communicating special requirements.
  • Ensure package labels agree with the booking, packing list, transport documents and any regulatory declaration.
  • For multiple packages, number them clearly (for example, 1 of 5, 2 of 5) and provide a matching package count list.

Never conceal required hazard labels or apply dangerous-goods marks unless the shipment is correctly classified, packaged, documented and accepted under the applicable rules.

7. Palletised and larger freight

Use a pallet or base with sufficient rated capacity, dimensions and condition for the load and handling equipment. Stack cartons squarely, distribute weight evenly and avoid overhang unless the provider has approved it. Keep the load stable and protect exposed edges.

  • Secure freight using suitable stretch wrap, strapping, corner boards or restraints; wrapping alone may not be sufficient.
  • Keep the load within the pallet footprint and maintain access to any required lifting points.
  • Do not block forklift pockets or obscure package labels, hazard marks or identification.
  • Keep the centre of gravity low and avoid unstable top-heavy arrangements.
  • Confirm whether the pallet is returnable, exchangeable, fumigated or subject to a specific material standard for cross-border movement.

For heavy or abnormal loads, obtain a load plan and confirm lifting equipment, vehicle capacity, securing method and any permits before collection. Never exceed vehicle, pallet, floor or lifting-equipment ratings.

8. Fragile, perishable, high-value and special-handling goods

Fragile goods may need custom crating, shock indicators, vibration isolation or professional packing. Perishable and temperature-sensitive goods may need validated insulation, coolant, active temperature control, monitoring and a defined excursion response. High-value goods may need secure packaging, restricted visibility, declared-value arrangements and special custody controls.

These services are not automatically included in a standard booking. Confirm the exact handling capability, monitoring, permitted temperature range, security arrangements, insurance position and provider acceptance in writing before dispatch. Do not use dry ice, refrigerants, chemical packs or other controlled materials without confirming applicable rules and acceptance.

9. Dangerous and restricted goods

Do not pack or tender dangerous goods as ordinary cargo. Batteries, aerosols, gases, flammable liquids, chemicals, oxidisers, corrosives and other regulated materials may require classification, approved packaging, marks, labels, declarations, segregation and trained handling. The rules differ by road, rail, air and sea and by jurisdiction.

Disclose the full contents before booking and wait for written, shipment-specific acceptance. A Safety Data Sheet, ordinary carton, warning sticker or previous successful shipment does not by itself authorise transport. Review the Prohibited & Restricted Cargo Policy.

10. Documents and accurate declarations

Prepare the documents required for the shipment and route. These may include a packing list, commercial invoice, purchase/value evidence, consignment note, permits, licences, certificates, customs declarations, dangerous-goods paperwork or special handling instructions. Requirements vary; this list is not exhaustive.

Describe contents accurately and consistently. Do not undervalue, misclassify, conceal, mislabel or use vague descriptions to avoid screening, duties or restrictions. For international movements, confirm exporter/importer responsibilities, customs data, origin, classification and destination requirements before collection.

11. Confirm package count, weight and dimensions

Measure the final packed shipment—not the unpacked product. Record the number of pieces, each package's length, width and height, gross weight and any non-stackable or special handling characteristics. Follow the provider's measurement method and unit requirements.

Incorrect dimensions or weight can affect vehicle allocation, handling equipment, pricing, load safety and acceptance. Do not estimate where accurate weighing or measurement is reasonably available. Tell KRIM/provider before pickup if the final packed details change from the quote.

12. Prepare for pickup and handover

  1. Have all packages ready at the agreed collection point, safely accessible and within the agreed pickup window.
  2. Ensure the person handing over cargo is authorised and can answer questions about the contents and package count.
  3. Keep the packing list, shipment reference and required documents available.
  4. Disclose any change, damage, shortage, seal issue or safety concern before loading.
  5. Confirm package count and visible condition with the collecting party where the process allows; record discrepancies on the handover document.
  6. Do not load cargo into an unverified vehicle or hand it to an unidentified person. Use the booking's verification process.

Loading, blocking, bracing and securing responsibilities must be confirmed in the accepted work order. Do not assume the driver will perform loading or specialist securing unless expressly agreed.

13. Photograph and retain a preparation record

Before collection, consider taking dated photographs of each package, labels, seals, pallet condition and the complete shipment. Keep a copy of the final packing list, measurements, declarations and handover record. For valuable or fragile goods, retain serial numbers and condition records.

These records can help resolve quantity, condition and handover questions. They do not guarantee that a claim will be accepted or establish liability by themselves. For incidents after pickup, follow the Shipment Claims & Damage Reporting Policy.

14. Common preparation mistakes to avoid

  • Using oversized cartons with empty space or undersized cartons that place pressure on the goods.
  • Leaving items loose, allowing internal movement or mixing heavy and fragile goods without separation.
  • Using weak, wet, damaged or previously contaminated packaging.
  • Applying inaccurate labels, leaving old routing labels visible or failing to identify multi-piece shipments.
  • Failing to disclose batteries, liquids, chemicals, high value, perishables or restricted contents.
  • Providing estimated or inconsistent package counts, weights, dimensions or values.
  • Assuming a quote, payment, driver arrival or past shipment confirms acceptance of changed cargo.
  • Failing to check access, loading equipment, permits, time windows or site restrictions.

15. Final pre-dispatch checklist

  1. Goods are lawful, correctly described and eligible for the proposed route and mode.
  2. Any restricted or dangerous cargo has written shipment-specific approval.
  3. Packaging is sound, suitable, closed and secure for the expected journey.
  4. Contents are cushioned, immobilised and protected against foreseeable handling conditions.
  5. Labels, addresses, package numbers and handling marks are accurate and legible.
  6. Package count, gross weight, dimensions and cargo value are accurate.
  7. Required invoices, packing lists, permits and declarations are ready.
  8. Pickup site, access, loading and handover arrangements are confirmed.
  9. Photos and copies of key records have been retained.
  10. Shipment-specific carrier instructions and work-order requirements have been followed.
Remember: Proper preparation reduces avoidable risk but cannot eliminate all transit risk or guarantee a particular delivery outcome.
